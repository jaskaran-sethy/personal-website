I have come across this sentiment of being excessively injury-avoidant, especially among the fitness spaces. Now, certainly, there is some merit to it when you look at boxing and american football having a devastating percentile of chronic head trauma injuries. However, I will speak to my experience as it stands to recovering from injury from the gym and from real life. Aggressive sports and vehicle accidents, however, are something I have no basis to speak from.

So a little background about me. My injury history in the past meager 3-5 years of training and life has been: impinged shoulders, tremendous knee tendonitis, excessive elbow tendonitis, hamstring tendonitis, pec strain, wrist sprains (and tendonitis), ankle sprains, and disc herniation (and the resulting sciatica). I have recovered a 100% from all of these injuries through self-conceptualized training, all while suffering from chronic asthma and allergies (and a couple bouts of hospitalization-needed pneumonia). The sciatica was by far the least fun of them, but I no longer have to worry about it.

I would have loved to not have to have self-treated myself through them, and I tried relying on orthopedics and physiotherapists at the beginning of every injury before quickly realizing that I was making a mistake yet again. See, orthopedics are at least justified: their philosophy is (well, usually) not to have you be able to squat 160kg when you got injured squatting 140kg. They just want to make you a normal, functional human being who can do a few body weight squats. To this end, they are excellent, and they will get you in that position.

However, if you are like me, that sounds like a nightmare. I needed my performance back; and not just that, but I wanted to get stronger. Here within perhaps you can recall a few instances you might have heard of news cycles or sports cycles of athletes being declared "can never play again" being completely fine in 1-2 years of rehab. Effective sports rehab is THE solution to becoming stronger than you were when you got injured.

Unfortunately, and I do not want to generalize this for most of India, perhaps it is my particular affordability band and the regions I look in -- physiotherapists are useless. They will take your money to run electrical signals and ultrasounds through your injury and call it a "necessary healing mechanism" due to promoting blood flow. Dude, if you want more blood flow in that area, just move it -- things can't move in your body without blood actively providing them the energy and oxygen to. Or, if you are lazy, just put a hot pack on it.

Anyway, I do not want to err on the side of giving advice on HOW to recover from injury. I will mention what works for me -- this might not work for you. I also do not want to err on the side of discounting all professionals in these fields, there are some very excellent and competent people in the industry; just not people I have easy or affordable access to.

Consider it a fact of life that you WILL get injured. It's about WHEN it happens, not IF. However, you can largely end up controlling the environment in which you get injured. Either you injure yourself in daily life picking up a brick off the floor, or you injure yourself deadlifting 200 kilos. Believe it or not, the severity of the injury in either case does not end up correlating much to how impressive the feat seems -- and the recovery timeline for either ends up being about the same depending on the grade of the injury.

Generally, when a body part gets injured, its due to one of two reasons:

1. It was weak, you depending a total stress which took most of your body's tissues to their limits, and it broke since it was the weakest link.
2. It was extremely strong and load bearing. Since the human body has a lot of built-in redundancy, it finally broke when it could no longer cope due to the rest of your body throwing all their weight behind it.

![Two chains under load. In the first, the thinnest link in the middle breaks. In the second, the middle link is the thickest, carrying the load while the links around it hang slack, and it cracks.](/blog/injury/two-reasons.svg "Reason 1 breaks the weakest link. Reason 2 breaks the one everything else leaned on.")

So for example, usually when I injure my wrists from benching, it's reason #1. And when I herniated my lower back, it was reason #2. Having some system for figuring out which reason my injury happened determines my entire approach to training it to get back to full performance. Especially if it is reason #2, I end up needing to push a lot of hypertrophy and strength towards the things that failed and ended up having my stronger parts take over -- so I can relearn how to use those "weak" muscles my body subconsciously started taking load off of.

Regardless, there are three guiding principles that I believe in.

1. You need to do the exercise or movement pattern that got you injured after 3-5 days of rest. Start small and weak, in a way that just gets blood flowing in the area and prevent the rigid formation of scar tissue. Try to get your body mobile on the injured part again, even if it kind of hurts to do that.
2. You need to progress the training on that movement pattern; you absolutely need to get back to how strong you were before or you will never feel 100% again.
3. If your injury occurred due to your muscle being way too load bearing, you need to push hard on the weak links that caused the load bearing in the first place.\
   If your injury occurred due to your muscle being weak, double the volume of training for it and START EATING. It needs to get big and strong so it never considers breaking again.
4. Fix your programming to see if load management was properly distributed amongst muscle groups or not, and if it was, whether single day volume was too much.

Following these principles, NEVER OPENING REDDIT ABOUT AN INJURY (This is genuinely worse than opening WebMD for a headache and having it determine you have cancer), and having BLIND TRUST that you will be okay within 3-6 months as long as you keep doing these three things will sort basically everything out.

One caveat; and this one really does entail why I feel following pre-existing models of how others treat the same injury you got might not work; my disc herniation was not getting better by squatting, deadlifting or barbell rows -- the patterns that broke it in the first place. This was the first experience I've had of facing something not following the pattern. I tried splashing around for answers but had to live through about a year of misery. Finally, I was able to restart experimenting, and I found godlike equipment that treated my entire pain pattern completely - 45 degree roman extensions machine (started from 20kg, progressed to 80kg and I was fixed) and doing cable deadlifts on the cable row machine. My back is stronger than it has ever been.

Most injuries, including mild-moderate muscle and tendon tears, can be treated just by picking the right set of moments for the right amount of sets and reps (what I would call ACTUAL sports physio). You will be fine, everyone gets injured: you're fine unless you get melted off. Stop watching Physio-Turned-Influencers on youtube in the business of getting clicks who will find any esoteric reason to call your technique wrong. Follow standardized technique, but make sure to adapt it for what feels good for you instead of what a physio said. Technique is a very, small miniscule part of getting injured -- it usually revolves around poor programming or poor nutrition.

Don't consider injuries a death sentence. They let you focus on the rest of your body for a bit -- enjoy that extra forearm session you could not fit in before. Fitness is a long term game, getting worried about a single limb possibly partially not working inhibiting your entire fitness experience seems a bit more dysfunctional to me than for normal human beings.
