When I joined Sprinklr, a book called 'Mindset' was part of the welcome package. Graduating fresh out of a premier engineering college where studying was done primarily via online notes, recordings and .ppt files rather than real books, I had fallen out of the habit of reading a physical copy. Reading Dweck's work made me realize that I had been missing out on a substantial amount of knowledge and insight that is fairly inaccessible unless you go out looking for it. Mindset ended up being the most influential book I read in 2023, and has completely transformed my default outlook on life and skill acquisition. It was easy to attribute things that I was "naturally bad" at as not having the 'talent for it', 'being incompatible', etc. whereas the things I was "naturally good" at were genetic gifts.

While I wouldn't fully disagree with that even now -- this book made me realize that genetic factors really only matter if you're trying to get to the very top of a field. You can get really, really brilliant just through sheer hard-work and grit. The moat of talent only presents itself in the amount of time you need to invest in getting good at the task, as well as reaching the very pinnacle of it. With enough dedication, an average human being can achieve a lot more than they could've ever initially dreamed of. Inadequacies can be treated as being borne out of intention; rather than truths that just exist. When you put yourself in the frame of accepting that you aren't good at particular things since you haven't prioritized them, your outlook on what is achievable changes drastically -- especially when you do realize how much subconscious work you might have put in the things you were "naturally good" at.

The book is inherently very preachy; and keeps repeating the same argument and concepts again and again; just interspersed with real life evidences between them to keep it fresh. One could argue the book shouldn't have been longer than 70 pages long, but perhaps the impact it could create on a reader's mind would've lessened. I do believe in quality over quantity, but there is hypnotic value to repetition.

The thesis essentially boils down to this: People who believe in a "Growth Mindset", as in, they can improve and become one of the greats at ANYTHING they put their mind to, tend to be happier and achieve a lot more in life; than the people who believe in a "Fixed Mindset" and believe they have attributes they are tuned to and attributes that they would never pick up in a million years. According to numerous well-conducted research studies, the things we take to be "fixed variables", such as IQ, musical talent, natural athleticism, are all indistinguishable from sufficient hard work in their fields. As long as you are willing to "Grow" into any path, you will put yourself in an incredible position to actually achieve it. Everything is a learnable skill.

Unfortunately, the book does present its ideas with factual aplomb that is not merited, especially in fields such as sociology and psychology where scientific understanding is still undergoing rapid change. However, from my own life after having read and implemented the ideologies present, I can completely understand why the author has accepted these things as absolute fact if she has lived through them -- it ends up feeling so obvious once you experience it.

The fact that such great literature exists out there was a strong motivator for me to get back into reading, and I would highly recommend this book despite the nits I do have with it. Some people have already had to work hard to get to where they were, starting very early in life, so this book might be essentially useless for them since that is a hard lesson they have learned first hand. However, for people like me, to whom some things have come naturally and others not so much, and having chosen to specialize in those natural things -- it's a huge wake up call to the realm of possibilities that is life.
